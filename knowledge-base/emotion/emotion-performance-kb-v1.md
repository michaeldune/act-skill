# Emotion Performance Knowledge Base

> Performance Skill starter knowledge base v1.0. Used to turn abstract emotion words into actor behaviour that can be seen, heard and executed second by second.

## Principles of Use

- Understand the character, the relationship, the objective and the trigger first, then look up entries; the knowledge base does not replace story judgement.
- A beat usually takes only one primary channel and one supporting channel; avoid having every reaction happen at once.
- Emotion and performance always take up 60-70% of the prompt; camera, continuity and model parameters are only brief support.
- When no entry fits, generate the behaviour yourself from the character's motivation; do not force an entry just to use the knowledge base.

## Performance Generation Chain

**External objective + internal suppressed objective -> inciting trigger -> physiological and attention shift -> visible facial/body action -> voice or silence -> unfinished action -> residue at the end**

## 1. Sadness: Restraint, Delayed Reaction and the Edge of Breaking

**When to use:** Loss, farewell, rejection, realising something cannot be undone. The point is not to cry right away, but that the character still wants to stay functional or keep their dignity.

**Core conflict:** External objective: keep the conversation going, finish the task at hand, or leave. Internal objective: not let the other person see they have been hit.

### Stages and Visible Evidence

- **Reception:** After the trigger, a brief hold; the eyes stay on the original task; the breath does not immediately get bigger.
- **Holding it down:** The jaw or lower lip tightens for a moment, the blink rate changes; the hands keep working but slow down.
- **Leakage:** An extra pause appears in the middle of a line, the ends of phrases lose support; the eyes avoid the other person.
- **Residue:** Tears may well up but need not fall; the action stops unfinished, and the emotion stays in the breath.

### Voice, Body and Camera

- **Voice/breath:** The volume does not have to drop; first keep a normal pace, then let slight breaks, a swallow or a failed inhale appear just before or after the key word.
- **Body/weight:** The chest holds in; the shoulders need not visibly slump; the weight can stay on the foot that was about to leave but never steps.
- **Camera support:** Prefer a steady camera that observes the restraint; if it pushes in, start after the first leak in the performance and stop at a framing where the eyes or breath can be read.

### Forbidden Reactions

No tears before the trigger; no constant frowning or downturned mouth; no full-body trembling the instant bad news arrives.

### Ready-to-Use Prompt Fragment

> After hearing the trigger line, she still gets halfway through the task in her hands without looking up; her jaw tightens for just an instant, her breath catches before the next line, and she ends on the unfinished action.

## 2. Anger: Suppression, Defence and Short Outbursts

**When to use:** A boundary is crossed, humiliation, being misunderstood, or losing control. Anger is often used to protect a more vulnerable emotion.

**Core conflict:** External objective: make the other person stop, admit it, or back down. Internal objective: not reveal fear, hurt or helplessness.

### Stages and Visible Evidence

- **Alert:** After the trigger the muscles around the eyes tighten and the gaze locks onto the other person; the body goes still first rather than moving at once.
- **Control:** The lips press together or a short breath is pushed out through the nose; sentences get shorter; hand movement is deliberately restrained.
- **Breakthrough:** Only one action suddenly speeds up, such as looking up, turning, or slapping an object down; do not stack several at once.
- **Pulling back:** After the outburst comes a quieter aftermath: heavier breathing, eyes moving away, or the voice pressed back down.

### Voice, Body and Camera

- **Voice/breath:** Suppressed anger can be very quiet with sharper diction; the outburst lasts only a short moment, leaving panting or silence afterwards.
- **Body/weight:** Weight shifting forward means closing in, shifting back means defending; choose one meaning for the relationship, do not charge and retreat at the same time.
- **Camera support:** During the control stage the camera can stay still and keep the distance between the two; only when the relationship crosses a line or the outburst comes does a slight follow or change of position kick in.

### Forbidden Reactions

No shouting from start to finish; no smashing things without motivation; do not equate anger with shaky handheld camera.

### Ready-to-Use Prompt Fragment

> He stops completely first, his eyes fixed on the other person's face; he presses his lips together, finishes the short line in a lower voice, and only after the last word suddenly presses the object back down onto the table, then does not move again.

## 3. Fear: Freezing, Scanning and Survival Response

**When to use:** A threat not yet confirmed, danger closing in, a secret about to be exposed. First decide whether the character freezes, flees, fights, or appeases.

**Core conflict:** External objective: confirm the danger and find a safe way out. Internal objective: not let the threat notice that they have sensed it.

### Stages and Visible Evidence

- **Something off:** A change in a sound or an object comes first; the rhythm of the character's action breaks slightly.
- **Scanning:** The eyes move first, the head follows; breathing gets shallow and listening sharpens.
- **Choosing:** The weight turns toward the exit, cover, or the source of the threat; the hand looks for something to rely on.
- **Action:** Pick only one main reaction, freeze, back away or step toward it, and keep the alertness that has not faded.

### Voice, Body and Camera

- **Voice/breath:** The voice can suddenly cut out, drop in volume, or the lines get shorter; or a silent inhale comes before speaking.
- **Body/weight:** Freezing is not total rigidity: the eyes and the breath are still searching; before fleeing, the character usually first settles on a direction and sets their weight.
- **Camera support:** While the threat is unconfirmed, keep a limited field of view and partial obstruction; change the framing or follow only after the character confirms the threat.

### Forbidden Reactions

The character must not look at a door before it makes a sound; no heavy panting from the first second; no repeated glances over the shoulder to fake tension.

### Ready-to-Use Prompt Fragment

> The footsteps outside the door suddenly stop; her hand is still resting on the rim of the cup, her eyes shift toward the door first while her head does not turn right away; her breathing gets shallow, her weight slowly moves to her back foot, and only after the second click of the lock does she step back half a pace.

## 4. Shame: Making Yourself Smaller and Avoiding Being Seen

**When to use:** Being found out, failing in public, being forced to admit vulnerability. Shame is about "how others see me", which is different from simple guilt.

**Core conflict:** External objective: end the attention, change the subject, or restore surface order. Internal objective: hide self-rejection and the fear of being cast out.

### Stages and Visible Evidence

- **Exposure:** Realising they are now the centre of attention, the eyes briefly lose anywhere to land.
- **Shrinking:** The head and neck pull in slightly and the body takes up less space; the hands tidy things that do not need tidying.
- **Covering:** An ill-timed little laugh appears, too much explanation, or an answer that is suddenly very short.
- **Withdrawal:** The body leaves the relationship first, then looks for a practical reason to leave.

### Voice, Body and Camera

- **Voice/breath:** Phrase endings fall and the volume narrows; or the pace briefly speeds up, using a flood of information to cover the emotion.
- **Body/weight:** Avoid eye contact with everyone; the weight pulls inward, and the shoulders do not slump dramatically but reduce the character's presence.
- **Camera support:** Keeping the spatial pressure of the people around is enough; no need for an exaggerated high angle; the character can sit at the edge of frame or be slightly blocked by the foreground.

### Forbidden Reactions

Do not play shame as continuous crying; no unmotivated head-down stillness for the whole shot; do not let a humiliating high-angle shot become the main point.

### Ready-to-Use Prompt Fragment

> When the conversation turns to her, her eyes first search the tabletop for somewhere to land, then she looks down and straightens the corners of papers that are already neat; she gives a small laugh with no humour in it, answers too quickly, and finally uses reaching for her water as a way to move her body out of everyone's sight.

## 5. Guilt: Self-Blame, Repair and Not Daring to Come Close

**When to use:** The character believes their actions have hurt someone. Compared with shame, guilt is more about "what I did wrong" and how to repair it.

**Core conflict:** External objective: apologise, make amends, or accept the consequences. Internal objective: afraid the apology will be refused, and afraid to face the evidence of the harm.

### Stages and Visible Evidence

- **Facing it:** Seeing the person who was hurt or the consequences, the action slows down and the words they had prepared will not come for a moment.
- **Approaching:** The body intends to move forward, but the steps or the reaching hand stop at a safe distance.
- **Admitting:** Avoid long self-justification; a pause before the key line, the voice still audible.
- **Waiting:** After speaking, do not immediately demand a response; bear the other person's silence.

### Voice, Body and Camera

- **Voice/breath:** The lines can shift from explaining to directly taking responsibility; the key is not a tearful voice but stopping the self-defence.
- **Body/weight:** Unfinished approaches, such as reaching out and pulling back, putting an object back where it was, or stopping at the doorway, show the boundary of the relationship.
- **Camera support:** The camera should keep the distance between the two visible; when needed, stay on the reaction of the person who was hurt rather than only following the one apologising.

### Forbidden Reactions

Do not substitute repeated apologies for action; do not admit fault while pressuring the other person to forgive; the character's emotion must not upstage the person who was hurt.

### Ready-to-Use Prompt Fragment

> He stops two steps away, and the hand he had raised never touches her; the explanation he had prepared disappears before the first line, he says only a short line taking responsibility, then stays quiet without pressing her to look at him.

## 6. Longing and Loss: Triggered by Everyday Objects

**When to use:** Someone is absent, a relationship has ended, an old space still holds traces. The emotion is often stirred unexpectedly in the middle of an everyday task.

**Core conflict:** External objective: carry on with daily life or sort through belongings. Internal objective: wants to get close to the memory, but fears being overwhelmed by it.

### Stages and Visible Evidence

- **Routine:** The character does a familiar task at normal speed, without playing the sadness in advance.
- **Trigger:** An object, smell or sound appears; the action stops first, the expression changes a little later.
- **Approaching:** The fingers touch the edge of the object or hover over it, not necessarily picking it up.
- **Returning:** The character goes back to the task, but the rhythm has changed, and the memory lingers in small actions.

### Voice, Body and Camera

- **Voice/breath:** Can be without dialogue; if they speak, it is like confirming a fact to an empty room or to the memory, at natural volume, not forced into poetry.
- **Body/weight:** Eyes and hands work better than large body movement; let a practised action briefly lose its flow.
- **Camera support:** First place the object and the character in the same space, then choose to stay on the character's reaction or shift focus slightly; do not point out the trigger for the audience too early.

### Forbidden Reactions

No clutching an old object and sobbing the moment it is seen; no overuse of slow motion; do not replace performance with a pile of flashbacks.

### Ready-to-Use Prompt Fragment

> She puts away two cups as usual, and only stops when her hand touches the rim of the second; her eyes stay inside the cupboard, the pad of her finger brushes lightly along a chip, and a few seconds later she puts the cup back in its place, but she closes the cupboard door more slowly than before.

## 7. Joy and Relief: Released Only After Confirmation

**When to use:** Waiting for a result, a misunderstanding cleared up, danger passed, a reunion. Real relief usually confirms safety first, then lets the body let go.

**Core conflict:** External objective: confirm the news is real and respond to others. Internal objective: does not dare believe too early, to avoid being disappointed again.

### Stages and Visible Evidence

- **Disbelief:** The gaze holds, the body has not yet moved closer; first searching whether the other person is joking.
- **Confirmation:** Only when a second piece of evidence appears does the breath truly release, and the shoulders and chest loosen.
- **Approaching:** A smile slowly forms around the eyes or at the corners of the mouth; the steps quicken but keep the spatial logic.
- **Afterglow:** The smile does not have to keep growing; it can settle into a hug or a quiet look between them.

### Voice, Body and Camera

- **Voice/breath:** The first line may be very soft or a repeated check; after confirmation the pace speeds up and the voice brightens, or there is only a laughing exhale.
- **Body/weight:** Going from frozen to loosening has more layers than jumping up straight away; the move toward the other person must come after confirmation.
- **Camera support:** At the moment of confirmation the camera can pull back slightly to hold the two characters' relationship, or stay close to read the relief; movement is triggered by a change in the relationship.

### Forbidden Reactions

No smiling before the news arrives; no exaggerated cheering throughout; before a hug, do not ignore the distance and obstacles between the two.

### Ready-to-Use Prompt Fragment

> After hearing the result she does not smile right away, she first watches the other person to make sure; only when the second nod comes does she let out the breath she has been holding, her shoulders loosen, a smile slowly appears around her eyes, and then she steps closer.

## 8. Jealousy and Insecurity: Watching, Comparing and Covering Up

**When to use:** Attention in a relationship shifts elsewhere, status is threatened, fear of being replaced. Can show up as excessive politeness, fault-finding or pretending not to care.

**Core conflict:** External objective: keep up appearances, confirm the relationship, or win back attention. Internal objective: not let anyone see their insecurity.

### Stages and Visible Evidence

- **Catching it:** The eyes briefly follow the interaction between the other two, then deliberately look away.
- **Comparing:** The task at hand becomes overly precise, or the posture shifts slightly into competition.
- **Covering up:** The tone is polite but the pauses fall in unnatural places; the smile arrives late or vanishes fast.
- **Probing:** Asks a seemingly ordinary question that actually checks the relationship; after asking, watches the other person's reaction.

### Voice, Body and Camera

- **Voice/breath:** Avoid an openly "sour" tone; let one word get slight emphasis, or let a polite line harden at the end.
- **Body/weight:** Avoid staring at anyone for long; small moves to claim space, edge closer or fix one's own appearance can serve as supporting evidence.
- **Camera support:** The framing can briefly hold the three-way relationship or the spatial distance; no need for quick cuts to close-ups to explain every glance.

### Forbidden Reactions

No constant eye-rolling or lip-curling; do not equate jealousy directly with malice; do not have every character see through it immediately.

### Ready-to-Use Prompt Fragment

> Her eyes linger only for an instant when the two lean closer, then she looks down and arranges the coasters far too neatly; when she looks up the smile arrives half a beat late, she asks an ordinary question in a very polite tone, and after asking she quietly watches the other person.

## Suggested Search Term Combinations

| Dimension | Examples |
|---|---|
| Emotion | sadness / anger / fear / shame / guilt / longing / relief / jealousy |
| Stage | reception / holding it down / leakage / confirmation / outburst / pulling back / residue |
| Intensity | slight / restrained / moderate / short outburst / edge of breaking |
| Behavioural tendency | freeze / avoid / approach / defend / repair / scan / cover up |
| Relationship constraint | does not want to be seen / must not disturb others / must keep up appearances / afraid of being disappointed again |

## Standard for New Entries

Every new entry must include: when to use, the dual objective, a clear trigger, at least three stages, facial/body/voice evidence, residue at the end, forbidden reactions, brief camera support and one ready-to-use prompt fragment. If it has only an abstract definition and no executable action, it is not included.
