## 00:03:50-00:05:10

**Spoken audio (verbatim):**

Fourth, add supporting calibration. Next comes the most professional layer of this prompt: AUs. AUs come from FACS, the Facial Action Coding System. It does not directly judge whether someone is sad, angry or afraid; instead it breaks an expression down into action units, movements on the face that can be observed. For example, AU1 is the inner brow raising, AU4 is the brow lowering, AU5 is the upper eyelid raising, AU7 is the eyelids tightening, AU15 is the lip corners pulling down, and AU17 is the chin raising and tensing. Each action can also have an intensity from A to E. A is just barely noticeable, and E is near the limit of the facial movement. Expressions also do not appear all at once; professional acting watches three phases: the action begins, the action reaches its peak, and the action gradually fades.

For example, when someone is angry, you should not pull every muscle to maximum from the first frame. Instead, start with AU4 brow lowering, AU7 eyelid tightening and AU23 lip tightening. Only when they reach a certain provoking word do AU25 and AU26 suddenly intensify, so the lips part and the jaw drops, forming a short outburst, and after the line it quickly subsides. That is how you get the process of holding it in, losing control, and regaining control.

But AUs are not an emotion formula. AU4 brow lowering, combined with clenched teeth, a hard stare and leaning forward, may read as anger; combined with the inner brow raising, the eyes dropping and the mouth corners sinking, it may become pain. So don't expect to toss a string of codes into the prompt, and the model may not reliably parse every AU anyway. The most stable approach is to first write the visible actions in natural language, then use AUs and intensities as supporting calibration.

The video explains how to translate abstract emotions such as "sadness, anger, shame" into the timing, dialogue, facial actions, gaze, body, voice, breath, pauses and prohibitions that AI video models can execute more easily, and organizes this into a three-layer structure and an eight-dimension formula.

## 3. Full content summary

The point is not to make the prompt longer, but to compile the director's intent into executable events. The global layer locks the characters, setting, voice, style and continuity; the shot layer specifies whom to watch and when to cut; the performance layer binds the character's objective, emotional change, dialogue triggers, facial and body actions, breath, pauses and forbidden reactions to specific times.

A performance cannot hold one "sad face" from start to finish. In this example, the woman defends herself with anger first, then justifies herself; then the defense cracks, her voice breaks, and finally she moves into confession. Dialogue is not just text to be spoken; it is also the timeline for action. FACS/AU can help calibrate the direction and intensity of actions, but it cannot replace natural language, and it is not an emotion formula. Actions need to go through onset, peak and fade.

# Part 4: Complete New Version, Optimized from the Original

At the time I recorded this, the dashboard showed about 4.12 million views and 242,000 likes for this video. The task charge shown on screen is 780 credits. I used one very long performance prompt to generate an emotionally intense AI two-hander. A lot of viewers suspected I had fed in the original video and only replaced the background and the people. My approach was: no reference video, no stack of character or location reference assets; instead, I wrote the directing, camera and performance requirements into one structured prompt.

This one case cannot prove that any prompt is guaranteed to succeed on the first try, but it does show this: compared with rerolling over and over with vague adjectives, breaking the performance down into executable instructions raises the usability of first-pass results. You can tell the AI actor when to pause, when to take a breath, on which word the voice breaks, when the eyes are only allowed to well up, and when the first tear is finally allowed to fall.

Why do so many people still reroll their AI videos over and over, while a structured prompt can make the performance more controllable? Because it does not just tell the AI "she is sad"; it spells out all the information an actor needs to play this scene well.

In the past, most AI videos were only a few seconds long. They could generate someone crying, laughing or raging, but struggled to actually perform a scene. Now long-duration models understand complex instructions better, and 30 seconds gives a character room to go through the build-up, the turn and the collapse of an emotion. But a longer clip also amplifies character drift, lip-sync errors, continuity mistakes and runaway camera movement, so structured control matters even more.

The whole performance prompt can be split into three layers.

Layer one is global control. It covers duration, setting, era, the characters' looks, costume, voice, and character consistency across shots. Visual style also belongs to this layer. If the platform already has a style preset close to your target film, you can let it carry the general visual tone and leave more space for the characters' performance. But the key lighting that affects the performance still has to be written out separately: whether there is a catchlight in the eyes, which side of the face is in shadow, when tears catch the light, and whether the background exposure will swallow the silhouette. A style preset cannot replace camera direction.

Layer two is the shot timeline. First split the scene into shots, and state which second each shot runs from and to, whom the camera is mainly watching, and when the angle changes. Don't write a flat list of timecodes. Organize by shot first, then put the emotion timecodes inside the matching shot: the shot is the parent structure, and the emotional beats are subsets inside it.

Layer three is the real performance control. It has to answer: What does the character want to do at this moment? From what state is the emotion moving, and toward what? Which line, which word, triggers the change? How do the brows, eyes, nostrils, lips, jaw, gaze and body move while speaking? Where is the breath, where is the pause? What state does the character go into after speaking? Which reactions must not happen early? The first two layers build the set and keep the characters stable; only the third layer turns the performance into executable instructions.

Next, don't write "the woman cries angrily" straight away; break down the emotional beats first. Take her shot from 3.7 to 23.0 seconds: from 3.7 to 6.5 seconds, she fights back immediately; from 6.5 to 11.5 seconds, she starts proving her sacrifice; from 11.5 to 16.5 seconds, cracks appear in the anger, her eyes may well up, but the tears must not fall yet; from 16.5 to 20.0 seconds, her voice breaks clearly for the first time; from 20.0 to 23.0 seconds, she moves from accusation into an almost shame-filled confession.

In this scene, anger is her protective layer. If the prompt only says "cries angrily", the model may serve up the anger and the tears together from the first frame, and the emotion has no process. This character's Emotional Arc should be: anger holds -> protective layer cracks -> voice breaks -> sadness appears. This is the dramatic logic of this character, not a universal law for everyone.

The expression also has to genuinely connect with the dialogue. The wrong way is: "The woman says, very sadly", and then paste the whole block of dialogue underneath. All the AI can do then is hold a sad face while reading the sentences out. The right underlying idea is to treat the dialogue as the timeline for changes in expression, voice and body.

A performance prompt should spell out: the state before the line, the trigger line, the stressed word, eyes and body, breath and pause, and the state after the line. For example, she says: "What about my life? What about me?" The first sentence is louder, and she still looks straight at the man, because at this moment she is still demanding an answer. Between the two sentences there is a short, sharp intake of breath, and her eyes drop away for less than half a second. The second sentence is clearly quieter and slower. She looks back at the man, but the aggression has gone out of her eyes. In the setup of this scene, the dramatic job of this pause is to show her deciding whether to say the most vulnerable line. To let the model show that decision, write out the intake of breath, the eyes dropping, the return to eye contact and the aggression draining away. A pause is not an absence of acting; it uses breath, gaze and stillness to present what is left unsaid.

If you also want supporting calibration for facial actions, you can use AUs. AUs come from FACS, the Facial Action Coding System. It does not directly judge whether someone is sad, angry or afraid; it breaks an expression down into observable actions. AU1 is the inner brow raiser, AU4 the brow lowerer, AU5 the upper lid raiser, AU7 the lid tightener, AU15 the lip corner depressor, and AU17 the chin raiser (mentalis). Action intensity can be marked A to E, from just visible to near the limit of the action.

Each action also needs a time course: Onset, the action begins; Apex, the action reaches its peak; Offset, the action gradually fades. When someone is angry, they should not pull every muscle to maximum from the first frame. Start with AU4 brow lowering, AU7 lid tightening and AU23 lip tightening. Only on the provoking word do AU25 and AU26 suddenly intensify, the lips parting and the jaw dropping, forming a short outburst. Subsiding quickly after the line is what creates the "holding it in - losing control - regaining control" process.

But AUs are not an emotion formula. The same AU4 brow lowering, combined with clenched teeth, a hard stare and leaning forward, may read as anger; combined with the inner brows rising, the eyes dropping and the mouth corners sinking, it may be pain. Different models do not parse AUs reliably. The most dependable approach is: natural-language actions are the primary instruction, and AUs and intensities are only redundant calibration.

For sadness, for example, don't just write: "The woman is very sad, tears streaming down uncontrollably." Rewrite it as: "Her inner brows slowly rise, the corners of her mouth sink slightly, and her chin begins to tense. She opens her mouth as if to keep talking, but no sound comes right away. Her eyes gradually well up, but the tears must not fall yet. Supporting calibration: AU1, AU15 and AU17, slight increase."

For anger, don't just write: "The man roars loudly in anger." Rewrite it as: "Before speaking, he presses his lips together, clenches his jaw, and draws his brows inward and down. The first half of the line stays low. On the trigger word, his nostrils flare, his body leans forward, AU25 and AU26 intensify quickly, and the volume rises in a short burst. After the last word, he shuts his mouth immediately and regains control of his expression."

The model cannot automatically turn "inner shame" into one single correct image, but it can execute the evidence shame leaves behind: averted gaze, a tightened chin, the body pulling back slightly, the line ending losing its strength, a brief stillness after speaking. That is what translating a psychological word into visible evidence means.

These details are not made up out of thin air. First divide the face into five observation zones: brows, eyes and eyelids, nostrils, lips, and chin. Then observe four things: What is moving? In which direction? How strong is the action? Which line or which word triggers it? Record it in plain language first, then look up the AUs to calibrate, and finally bind it to a specific second and line. In one sentence: see it first, then name it; describe it in plain words first, then calibrate with AUs.

In the end, the AI actor performance prompt can be summed up in eight dimensions:

1. Time segment;
2. Character objective;
3. Emotional change;
4. Dialogue trigger word;
5. Facial action and AU;
6. Gaze and body;
7. Voice, breath and pause;
8. End state and forbidden reactions.

You can save this eight-dimension structure as a preset. When the same kind of subject comes up, reuse the structure rather than mechanically copying the content; swap in the character objective, dialogue triggers, timings and visible actions.

When you actually generate, go into Xiaoyunque AI, choose Seedance 2.5, and make the UI aspect ratio match the ratio in the prompt, for example both set to 16:9; set 30 seconds, turn on native audio, paste in the full prompt and submit. When the result comes back, don't just judge "does it look like a movie"; check it item by item against the eight dimensions: Are the characters consistent? Is the camera watching the right person at the right time? Does the trigger word set off the action? Are the breaths and pauses in sync with the dialogue? Does the first tear appear only after the specified time? Is the stillness after the line preserved?

If one item fails, change only one variable at a time. If the timing is wrong, fix the timecodes first; if the change did not happen, strengthen the trigger word and the natural-language action next; if the facial direction is off, use AUs and intensities as supporting calibration last. That is the only way to know which edit actually changed the result.

The essence of a performance prompt is not ordering the AI to "act better" with more flowery adjectives; it is writing the character's objective, triggers, actions, voice and prohibitions at each point in time as executable director's instructions. See it first, then name it; describe it in plain words first, then calibrate with AUs; break the emotion into beats first, then bind each beat to a line and a specific second. That is the key to taking an AI character from "striking an expression" to "completing a performance".
